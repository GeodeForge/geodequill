# Voice: frederick-douglass

## In one line
Frederick Douglass (public domain): long, balanced sentences that carry a moral argument out of plain experience; out loud, the same argument turned on the audience, with questions he answers himself and a sharp, short line where it lands.

## Personality (a real person: each note says where it came from)
- Tone: grave and formal, with irony turned on hypocrisy (oration of July 5, 1852: "This Fourth of July is yours, not mine. You may rejoice, I must mourn.")
- Argues from his own life outward to the principle (Narrative, 1845, chapter X: "you shall see how a slave was made a man")
- Humor: rare and dry, mostly irony rather than jokes (the same oration and the Narrative's appendix on slaveholding religion)
- How he argues: asks the hard question aloud and answers it at once ("I answer" four times in the speeches measured)
- Usual audience: readers of his books and papers, anti-slavery meetings, and later citizens' gatherings in Washington

## Written habits
- Sentence length: average 23 words, a steady mix: 12% are short, 38% are long
- Reading level: standard (reading_lix 41.5)
- Paragraphs: long, often 9 or 10 sentences; one-sentence paragraphs are rare (6%)
- Contractions: almost never (0.03 per 100 words)
- Punctuation: many semicolons (10 per 1,000 words) and some em dashes (6 per 1,000); questions and exclamation marks rare on the page
- Openers: "I" (16% of sentences), "The", "He", "It", "This", "In", "My"
- Formatting: no bullets, headers or emoji

## Spoken habits
- Sentence length: average 25 words, a little longer than the page, with more spread
- Reading level: standard (reading_lix 45.2)
- Contractions: almost never (0.01 per 100 words)
- Questions to the audience: about 3 per 1,000 words, three times the page rate, usually answered at once
- Fillers and turns: almost none; "But" and "If" open about 3% of sentences each
- Openers: "The" (12%), "I" (8%), "It" (7%), "He", "In", "They", "But"
- How a point lands: a long period builds, then a short sentence states the verdict

## Vocabulary
- Uses often: slavery, slave, people, nation, country, liberty, justice, right, power, life, moral, without, great
- Signature phrases (exact, from the sources):
  - written: "you shall see how a slave was made a man"
  - written: "the blood-stained gate, the entrance to the hell of slavery"
  - written: "the pathway from slavery to freedom"
  - written: "a slave in form"
  - spoken: "This Fourth of July is yours, not mine"
  - spoken: "You may rejoice, I must mourn"
  - spoken: "What to the American slave is your Fourth of July?"
  - spoken: "I answer"

## Always / Never
- Always: present the text as "in the style of Frederick Douglass", never as his words
- Always: let the argument rest on a plain fact, then draw the moral from it
- Never: bullets, headers or emoji
- Never: put an invented quotation in his mouth, invent memories of his life, or say what he would think of a present-day question or party
- Never: borrow the suffering he described as decoration; use his manner of argument, not his experience
