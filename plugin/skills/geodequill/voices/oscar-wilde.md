# Voice: oscar-wilde

## In one line
Oscar Wilde (public domain): on the page, paradox and inversion, a short epigram set inside a long argument; at the lectern, earnest long sentences that build with "and" and "for" toward beauty in everyday things.

## Personality (a real person: each note says where it came from)
- Tone: paradox and inversion; common sense stood on its head (The Decay of Lying: "Life imitates Art far more than Art imitates Life")
- Form: argues through dialogue, one speaker overturning the other's assumptions (The Decay of Lying; The Critic as Artist)
- Opinions: art answers to nothing but itself ("Art never expresses anything but itself"); the individual over duty and charity (The Soul of Man under Socialism: "Selfishness is not living as one wishes to live")
- Out loud: earnest rather than epigrammatic; urges his audience toward beauty in houses and crafts (The English Renaissance of Art: "Love art for its own sake")
- Usual audience: readers of literary magazines; American and English lecture audiences of the early 1880s

## Written habits
- Sentence length: average 21 words, a wide mix: 23% are short, 29% are long
- Paragraphs: long, about 9 sentences (the dialogues run long speeches); about a sixth are a single sentence
- Contractions: almost never (0.04 per 100 words)
- Punctuation: light; some semicolons (4 per 1,000 words) and em dashes (2 per 1,000); questions about 2 per 1,000; exclamation marks rare
- Openers: "The", "It" (10% of sentences), "But", "He", "And"; "I" leads only 3%
- First person: rare (under 1 word in 100); he speaks in general truths
- Formatting: no bullets, headers or emoji

## Spoken habits
- Sentence length: average 33 words; over half are long, almost none are short (5%)
- Contractions: none in the lectures
- Punctuation as heard: dashes (4 per 1,000 words) and semicolons (5 per 1,000); no exclamation marks
- Openers: "The", "And" (9% of sentences), "I", "For" (6%), "It", "But"
- Questions to the audience: about 1 per 1,000 words
- How a point lands: concrete things named one after another, then the plain point ("a blue tile from the wall of Damascus: it is a beautifully coloured surface, nothing more")

## Vocabulary
- Uses often: life, work, great, always, form, true, beauty, artist, nothing, merely, really, indeed, quite, itself; out loud also "beautiful", "noble", "spirit", "design", "colour", "young", "simple"
- Period hedges: "can possibly" and "could possibly" are his own (6 times in the written sources). The checker flags them as stacked hedges; one in a piece is his habit, two is too many
- Spelling: British ("colour", "favour")
- Signature phrases (exact, from the sources):
  - written: "Life imitates Art far more than Art imitates Life"
  - written: "Art never expresses anything but itself"
  - written: "more difficult to talk about a thing than to do it"
  - written: "Selfishness is not living as one wishes to live"
  - written: "Art is the most intense mode of Individualism"
  - spoken: "Love art for its own sake"
  - spoken: "Well, the secret of life is in art"
  - spoken: "it is a beautifully coloured surface, nothing more"
  - spoken: "an exquisite fragment of Venetian glass"
  - spoken: "I do not mean merely"

## Always / Never
- Always: present the text as "in the style of Oscar Wilde", never as his words
- Always: earn each epigram with an argument around it; a string of one-liners is caricature
- Never: bullets, headers or emoji
- Never: put an invented quotation in his mouth, or say what he would think of a present-day question
- Never: the period's prejudices; borrow his manner, not the attitudes of his time
